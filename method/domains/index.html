Non-circular domains

A Domain is the image of the unit disk under a map $z = f(\zeta)$. Functions on it are sampled at the mapped grid points Ω.z = f.(D.z), and the operators are pulled back to the disk:

\[\mathcal{S}_\Omega[u](f(\zeta)) = \frac{1}{4\pi} \int_D \frac{u(f(\eta))}{|f(\zeta) - f(\eta)|}\, J(\eta)\, d\eta,\]

with $J$ the Jacobian of the map. The singularity at $\eta = \zeta$ comes from the factor $1/|\zeta - \eta|$, and the rest of the kernel is smooth. Keeping the disk's PSH for the singular factor makes the operators on $\Omega$ as accurate as on the disk.

The weight is still the disk's, Ω.w $= \sqrt{1 - |\zeta|^2}$. Because the maps are smooth up to the boundary, it vanishes like the square root of the distance to $\partial\Omega$, which is the edge behavior of the densities on $\Omega$. Densities on a Domain are therefore the same forms as on the disk ($g/w$, $w g$).

Ellipses

ellipse(a, b, Mℓ, Mₘ) uses the affine map $f(\zeta) = a\,\mathrm{Re}\,\zeta + i\, b\,\mathrm{Im}\,\zeta$. Then $|f(\zeta) - f(\eta)| = \rho(\phi)\, |\zeta - \eta|$ exactly, with $\phi$ the angle of $\zeta - \eta$ and $\rho(\phi) = \sqrt{a^2\cos^2\phi + b^2\sin^2\phi}$. The single layer on the ellipse is the angular kernel $ab/\rho(\phi)$ on the disk, and the Stokes single layer is three angular kernels, the entries of

\[A_0(\phi) = \frac{ab}{\rho(\phi)}\left(\mathbf{I} + \hat\gamma\hat\gamma^T\right), \qquad \hat\gamma = \frac{(a\cos\phi,\, b\sin\phi)}{\rho(\phi)}.\]

All are sparse in coefficient space (see Angular kernels). They have more diagonals than the disk's, since $1/\rho(\phi)$ has infinitely many Fourier modes. The number kept is set by their decay to 1e-14, and grows as $b/a$ decreases. Building an ellipse is cheap: about 3 ms and 1.3 MiB at $M = 16$, and 0.02 s and 5 MiB at $M = 24$.

The ellipse also stores the Fourier coefficients of $A_0$ (Ω.ext, an EllipseGeometry), which the close evaluation between bodies uses.

Conformally mapped domains

Experimental

Conformally mapped domains are experimental: their accuracy and interface may change. The limits below (the origin must be inside the curve, slower convergence for elongated shapes, dense operators, no close evaluation between bodies) are known, and not yet addressed. For ellipses, use ellipse.

domain(γ, Mℓ, Mₘ) maps the disk conformally onto the inside of a closed curve. γ is the curve sampled at equispaced parameter values, counterclockwise. The map $f$ is computed from the samples in three steps:

  1. Solve an interior Dirichlet problem for a harmonic function with boundary values $-\log|\gamma|$, by a double-layer integral equation on the boundary.
  2. Take its harmonic conjugate, by a Cauchy integral.
  3. Evaluate $f$ and $f'$ anywhere inside by barycentric Cauchy integrals.

This normalizes $f(0) = 0$, so the origin must lie inside the curve. If it does not, domain returns without an error but its operators are wrong: a unit circle centered at $3$ gave a drag of $-3 \times 10^{-4}$ instead of $16/3$. The curve is upsampled by a factor of 5 by default, so it must be smooth and resolved by its samples.

With $M(\zeta, \eta) = (f(\zeta) - f(\eta))/(\zeta - \eta)$, which is smooth and nonzero with $M(\zeta, \zeta) = f'(\zeta)$, the single layer kernel on $\Omega$ is $1/(|M|\,|\zeta - \eta|)$. The package forms it as the disk's operator, the dense matrix $Q\,\hat K\,P$ of transform, eigenvalues and inverse transform, multiplied entrywise by the smooth factor $J(\eta)/|M(\zeta, \eta)|$. This is singularity swapping: the disk's operator absorbs the singularity, and the smooth factor is applied pointwise. The hypersingular operator and the Stokes blocks are built the same way. The Stokes blocks also include the angular factors $\cos^2$, $\sin\cos$, $\sin^2$ of the disk and the rotation by the angle of $M$.

The result is a set of dense $N \times N$ matrices, with $N$ the number of grid points, projected to coefficient space. Building them takes $O(N^2)$ memory and $O(N^3)$ time:
